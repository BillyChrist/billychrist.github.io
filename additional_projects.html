Lunar Whale

Autonomous VTOL Drone Testbed

Lunar Whale is an autonomous VTOL drone testbed built to investigate how distributed lifting surfaces, modular structures, and hybrid control architectures affect stability, controllability, and power efficiency across vertical and forward flight.

The platform was started as an iterative experimental vehicle for rapid design-build-test cycles. Future work focuses on expanded telemetry, quantitative flight validation, and architecture updates informed by collected test data.

Project Overview

The project was initiated as an experimental VTOL aircraft platform designed to rapidly test and iterate flight configurations, with a specific focus on improving passive stability and extending flight endurance.

Unlike a fixed-design vehicle, Lunar Whale was intentionally built as a reconfigurable testbed for evaluating how distributed lifting surfaces, airfoil selection, and control surface placement affect both stability and power consumption. Particular emphasis is placed on understanding whether passive aerodynamic features can reduce the need for continuous active control, thereby improving overall efficiency.

Development has been driven by rapid design-build-test cycles, enabling direct comparison between configurations. The long-term objective is to identify configurations that maximize flight time while maintaining stable and controllable behavior across both vertical and forward flight regimes.

  • Designed as an experimental platform for rapid iteration of flight configurations.

  • Primary focus on passive stability mechanisms and their impact on control effort.

  • Used to evaluate how airfoil geometry and lifting surface distribution affect power consumption.

  • Project objective: maximize flight endurance through aerodynamic and control optimization.

Airfoil Design

The lifting surfaces were designed around the NACA 4412 profile, selected using iterative comparison of airfoil performance data. Online tools such as airfoiltools.com were used throughout the design process to evaluate lift, drag, and stall characteristics across expected operating conditions.

The main wing was also configured with a 6 degree dihedral angle to introduce passive roll stability, improving lateral self-correction behavior during disturbances and reducing reliance on continuous active roll control.

  • Evaluated pitch range versus tilt transition behavior.

  • Performed iterative design and fabrication of test airfoils.

  • Used lightweight printed structures with treatment for surface quality and durability.

  • Referenced airfoiltools.com to evaluate and compare candidate airfoils during design iterations, guiding selection based on lift-to-drag behavior across relevant flight regimes.

Frame & Mechanical Design

The frame was developed as a modular mechanical system to enable quick swaps of lifting surfaces and subsystem layouts. Structural design choices were driven by repeated assembly, landing loads, and manufacturability with available processes.

The modular architecture allows controlled isolation of variables such as lifting surface placement and configuration, enabling direct comparison between test cases without full airframe redesign.

  • Designed modular interfaces for rapid reconfiguration and test turnaround.

  • Developed and validated tilt mechanisms for transition and control authority.

  • Balanced structural strength, weight, and durability across iteration cycles.

Avionics & Software

The system supports both manual pilot control and structured experimental flight testing, enabling repeatable evaluation of different aerodynamic configurations under consistent conditions.

  • Integrated flight controller, sensing, actuation, and communication subsystems.

  • Implemented low-level stabilization and actuator control for VTOL behavior.

  • Structured software to support rapid testing and iterative control tuning.

Test Strategy & Expected Outcomes

While full flight testing is still in progress, the platform was designed with a structured testing methodology to evaluate how configuration changes affect stability and power consumption.

The primary goal of testing is to determine whether passive aerodynamic features, such as aft lifting surfaces, can reduce control effort and improve overall flight efficiency.

  • Planned Test Comparisons:
    • Baseline configuration (no aft lifting surfaces)
    • Aft lifting surfaces at neutral incidence
    • Aft lifting surfaces at varying positive incidence angles

  • Key Metrics:
    • Total power consumption during steady forward flight
    • Required control input / actuator activity
    • Pitch stability and damping response
    • Trim condition and steady-state attitude

  • Expected Observations:
    • Improved passive stability with aft lifting surfaces
    • Potential reduction in control system workload
    • Trade-off between added drag and reduced control effort

The outcome of these tests will guide future airframe iterations, with the objective of identifying configurations that provide measurable improvements in flight endurance without sacrificing controllability.

Future Work
